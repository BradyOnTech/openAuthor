# Viewpoint and knowledge

Identify whose perception organizes the passage and the intended narrative distance. Filter detail through that person's experience, priorities and immediate attention when the voice calls for close perspective. More distant narration, filtering words and exposition can be deliberate; evaluate their effect against the book's intended voice.

Do not convert author-held secrets into a viewpoint character's thought. Knowledge can arrive through witnessed events, reported claims, deductions or approved canon states; those have different certainty. Preserve who is unsure about what and when. A reader's understanding may exceed the character's without making the narrator explain the secret.

Check whether judgments, imagery and vocabulary belong to the viewpoint or the established narrator. Characters may notice the same room differently; ground that difference in their evidence, not stereotypes. Avoid switching consciousness because another character's motive would make a paragraph easier to explain.

In critique, quote both the suspect wording and the evidence limiting knowledge when available. Distinguish an actual contradiction from a plausible interpretation or unavailable context. In revision, repair the knowledge leak without adding an event that teaches the character the missing fact.
