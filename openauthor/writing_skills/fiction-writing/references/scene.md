# Scene purpose and causality

Keep the physical sequence legible where it matters to a choice: who can reach an object, what has been opened or locked, when access ends, and what action makes the next one possible. Ordinary logistics can break a scene as readily as invented technology. Narrative compression is valid when the reader can still reconstruct a feasible sequence; do not explain every movement.

Honor an authored word range when supplied. Meet it by controlling the scene's duration and allocation of attention; do not pad with atmosphere or remove a necessary causal step. The host's counted requirement is separate from literary judgment.

Identify the scene's job in this story: a bargain, decision, test, rupture, realization, delay or deliberate pause. Establish what each relevant character wants now, the obstacle or incompatible aim, and what changes by the end. Use the author's purpose and intended turn; distinguish evidence from a proposed interpretation.

Choose beats connected by consequence. A character acts because of the previous pressure; the response changes available choices. A description, world rule or object earns space when it alters attention, access, risk, leverage or expectation. Do not equate activity with progression: moving equipment, looking away and nodding can leave an exchange unchanged.

An emotional turn should follow the character's particular choice or perception. Preserve mixed motives rather than replacing them with a neat moral lesson. A quieter scene may change the reader's understanding without ending in a confrontation. Do not force every scene into the same disaster template.

For an edit plan, name the specific weak beat, its consequence and the smallest useful intervention. Retain setup and existing strengths. For drafting, render the authored beats at a scale appropriate to their importance, then stop at the intended ending. Keep planning vocabulary out of the prose.
