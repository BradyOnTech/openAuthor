## The brake — action

### Source

The ferry struck the chain sideways. Mara felt the deck shudder under her feet and saw the winch begin to turn. The handle caught Ivo across the wrist. He made a small sound and sat down hard.

There were six passengers behind the rope. Six, and the boy who had crawled beneath it. Mara began to count them again, but the brake lever snapped back and she had to catch it with both hands. She had not reached the boy.

Ivo tried to stand. His right hand folded against his coat.

"Stay there."

"Splendid plan." He sounded annoyed about the weather.

The chain was running out. Beyond its last painted link the ferry would turn broadside to the sluice. Mara could hold the brake or reach the boy; the space between them was just wider than her body. She felt very frightened. She shouted at the nearest passenger to get him, but the woman only gripped the rope harder.

Mara let one hand leave the lever. The brake moved an inch. She put her hand back.

"Use your belt," Ivo said.

She pulled it free and threw the buckle end. It landed short of the boy, who watched it without moving. Mara drew breath to tell him what to do. The chain hit its last painted link.

Scene facts:

- Six passengers are behind the rope; the boy is additional and beneath it.
- Ivo's right wrist is injured; he suggests the belt, but cannot execute the rescue.
- Mara's recount is interrupted before she reaches the boy.
- The belt lands short; the boy is not rescued by the end.

Craft questions:

- Can you track the lever, chain and boy without rereading?
- Does fear emerge from Mara's constrained choice rather than extra emotion labels?
- Does Ivo's dry response remain different from Mara's commands?
- Does urgency vary sentence rhythm instead of making every sentence a fragment?

Calibration warning: Changing 'began to count' to a completed count changes an event even when every name and number survives.

### Sample A

The ferry struck the chain sideways. The deck shuddered under Mara’s feet. Ahead of her, the winch jerked into motion, and its handle swung across Ivo’s wrist. He made a small sound and sat down hard.

Six passengers stood behind the rope. Six—and the boy who had crawled beneath it. Mara started counting them again, forcing her eyes from one face to the next, but before she reached the boy, the brake lever snapped back. She caught it with both hands.

Ivo tried to stand. His right hand folded against his coat.

“Stay there.”

“Splendid plan.” He sounded annoyed about the weather.

The chain was running out. When the last painted link passed the winch, the ferry would swing broadside into the sluice. Mara could keep both hands on the brake, or reach the boy. The space between them was only a little wider than her body, but it might as well have been the river.

She looked at the boy. He was still beneath the rope, watching her. The nearest passenger had one hand on it. Mara shouted for the woman to get him. The woman only gripped the rope harder.

Mara let go with one hand. The lever shifted an inch. The ferry lurched beneath her. She caught the brake again.

“Use your belt,” Ivo said.

She pulled it free and threw the buckle end toward the boy. It struck the deck short of him. He watched it without moving.

Mara drew breath to tell him what to do.

The chain hit its last painted link.

“Get him!” she shouted at the nearest passenger.

The woman only gripped the rope harder. Her knuckles paled.

Mara let go with one hand. The brake shifted an inch beneath the other. The ferry answered with a low, dragging lurch. She seized the lever again.

“Use your belt,” Ivo said.

His voice came from the deck behind her. He could not do it himself; his injured hand remained closed against his coat.

Mara pulled the belt free. The buckle struck her palm, cold and heavy, and she threw it beneath the rope. It landed short of the boy.

He watched it without moving.

For one moment Mara saw the distance between them—not as space, but as the thing she had chosen each time she kept both hands on the brake. She drew breath to tell him what to do.

The chain struck its last painted link.

## The spare key — dialogue

### Source

Mara set a cup in front of Ivo. It was the one with the chipped blue rim, which meant she expected him to stay. He kept his coat on.

"Did you open the storehouse?" she asked.

"I opened a great many things yesterday. A drawer. An unfortunate letter."

She turned the cup until the chip faced him.

Ivo had hidden the spare key inside the bellows before dawn. She had seen him leave the quay, but the workshop window had been dark. Unless she had come back. He tried to remember whether the bellows had been hanging straight.

"The storehouse," she said.

"No."

That, at least, was true. He had given the other key to her brother. He wished she would ask where the key was. He wished she would ask almost anything except whether he had seen her brother.

"Then somebody used yours."

He lifted the cup with his left hand. The tea touched the sore place behind his teeth. He put it down without drinking.

"You could change the lock."

"I could."

She took off her own coat and hung it beside the door. Ivo looked at the empty peg beside it. He had come to return a favor and leave. Now she was taking bread from the tin, cutting two uneven slices as if the question had been settled.

Scene facts:

- Ivo hid the spare key in the bellows before dawn.
- Ivo did not open the storehouse; he gave the other key to Mara's brother.
- Ivo is unsure what Mara knows. Her private knowledge is not established.
- The conversation ends without confession or discovery of the spare key.

Craft questions:

- Can the speakers be distinguished without adding ornate dialogue tags?
- Do the cup and bread carry pressure rather than become explained symbols?
- Is Ivo's uncertainty maintained without narrating Mara's thoughts?
- Does humor function as evasion rather than an interchangeable quip?

Calibration warning: Saying Mara knows the key is in the bellows violates viewpoint and invents knowledge, although all lexical markers still pass.

“No.”

Not a question. He lifted the cup with his left hand. Tea touched the sore place behind his teeth. He set it down.

“You could change the lock.”

“I could.”

She took off her coat and hung it beside the door.

The empty peg beside hers pulled his eye. He had come to return a favor and leave. The door was still there. So was the cup.

Mara opened the bread tin and cut two uneven slices. No hurry. No explanation. She put one in front of him.

Ivo looked at it.

“You’re staying?”

“Apparently.”

“Good.”

She sat down with the other slice. He kept his coat on.

## The second place — introspection

### Source

Mara washed the second bowl before she remembered there was no need. She had already rinsed it once. A grain of rice clung beneath the lip, and she worked at it with her thumbnail until it came free.

Her father had been missing for eleven days. Missing was the word the harbor office used. It took up less room than the others. She could put it beside a date on a form and still have space for his height, the color of his coat, the scar she had never noticed until the clerk asked.

Outside, someone tested the evening bell. One note, a pause, another. For years she had complained that her father came home between the notes, when she was trying to count. She listened through the pause now.

The bowl slipped. She caught it against the side of the sink, leaving a white mark on the glaze. Not a crack. She ran her thumb across it twice.

On the table lay the letter she had written to his sister. She had not sealed it. The first line said he would be back by market day. Market day had been yesterday.

Mara dried both bowls. She put one in the cupboard and left the other on the table, covering it with a clean cloth. Then she crossed out the first line of the letter. She did not write another.

Scene facts:

- Mara's father has been missing for eleven days; his death is not established.
- Market day was yesterday; the letter has not been sealed.
- The bowl is marked but not cracked.
- She crosses out the first line without replacing it; one bowl remains covered on the table.

Craft questions:

- Does the shift from expectation toward uncertainty remain legible without naming a stage of grief?
- Are the repeated washing and checking purposeful rather than removed as redundancy?
- Does the final restraint survive the selected direction?
- Is imagery grounded in Mara's immediate objects instead of generic darkness, storms or shattered hearts?

Calibration warning: A faster version is not automatically better. Declaring the father dead would invalidate the scene even if the prose were elegant.
