# Varying dialogue without flattening the characters

The next useful experiment is to revise a short run of conversational moves before polishing individual lines. Both speakers can remain sharp. The question is whether each reply pursues that speaker's immediate purpose, responds to the actual pressure of the previous turn and changes the conversation. Varying sentence lengths alone would leave the same underlying exchange intact.

This note was researched on October 5, 2026. The sources are novelists' own craft discussions and a direct interview. Their methods support a testable writing-kit design; they do not demonstrate that an AI implementation will improve fiction.

**The local problem is a repeated pattern, not the presence of wit**

The author-feedback assessment (withheld from the public repo because it quotes a private manuscript) reports that focused feedback removed redundant interpretations but achieved little overall dialogue variation. Reading the focused candidate (withheld from the public repo because it quotes a private manuscript) narrows that diagnosis: the calibration exchange already has useful brief, direct answers. Elsewhere, an objection receives a polished rejoinder, followed by another equally finished response. The restricted-sample exchange keeps returning to that pattern even while the investigation advances.

The successful direct questions and answers are evidence against making every line longer, less witty or more hesitant. The experiment should target a particular run of replies, retain the technical investigation and preserve the credential bargain.

**Separate the scene's purpose from the speaker's purpose**

In Writing Excuses' dialogue masterclass, Maurice Broaddus and Mary Robinette Kowal distinguish the author's reason for including a line from the character's reason for saying it. Broaddus describes realizing during revision that rapid banter sometimes continues because he enjoys the characters rather than because the scene needs it. Howard Tayler also describes fixing an interview scene by changing its information sequence: characters exchange important revelations instead of letting one recount everything chronologically. Entertainment remains a legitimate purpose. [Writing Excuses: The Job of Dialogue](https://writingexcuses.com/17-29-the-job-of-dialogue/)

Our proposed adaptation is to label selected turns with an active verb and an object: obtain the sample index, defend the ordinary explanation, challenge the access restriction, concede a specific correction. Those labels describe existing source behavior; uncertain motives stay uncertain. If successive replies perform the same move and land with the same verbal flourish, identify one where a different delivery would better serve the unchanged goal. This is a design inference from the craft discussion, not a published AI procedure.

**Let the speakers follow different trains of thought**

George Saunders explains Doug Unger's dialogue advice as speakers talking from their own separate streams of thought, producing exchanges that can be oblique rather than perfectly linear. He also argues that dialogue must retain the energy of the surrounding prose. This supports a reply that picks up the detail its speaker cares about, rather than answering every clause of the previous line with a matching performance. It does not justify random non sequiturs. [Saunders interview with High Grade](https://highgrade.mines.edu/blog/art/2020/01/15/Interview.html)

For this chapter, ask which part of a question each man is actually willing or able to answer. A reply may answer the practical question plainly, answer only one part, insist on the unanswered request or decline to meet a joke with another joke. Choose according to the scene's evidence. A partial answer must not silently remove a factual claim, a concession, a condition or a challenge that the source requires.

**Make cadence specific to the relationship and the moment**

Kowal identifies three aspects of performed character voice that transfer to prose: pacing, sentence structure and attitude expressed through word choice. The same episode emphasizes that a character's purpose changes with the person addressed. It explicitly allows stylized, elaborate banter. Its interruption example distinguishes cutting exactly on a charged word for emphasis from letting the speaker continue slightly beyond it for a more natural timing effect. [Writing Excuses: Writing Conversational Dialogue](https://writingexcuses.com/18-28-writing-conversational-dialogue/)

Apply this through approved character examples and local pressure. Examine whether a speaker qualifies a claim, piles up subordinate clauses, uses a bare imperative, returns to an unanswered question or ends with a completed judgment. Avoid assigning one character perpetual fragments and another perpetual long sentences. A character can become more direct when precision matters, or more elaborate when protecting status. Those changes need support from the passage rather than a general character stereotype.

Patricia C. Wrede demonstrates that punctuation, repetitions, sentence grouping and the position of speech tags can change a line's perceived delivery. She distinguishes fictional speech from a transcript and gives an example of using meter to distinguish a character despite modern vocabulary. Her examples also show why cadence edits need meaning checks: altered delivery can reverse the apparent response to an event. [Wrede: Dialog](https://pcwrede.com/pcw-wp/dialog/)

A cadence pass should therefore name its intended effect before changing punctuation: clipped precision, reluctant acknowledgment, insistence or an unfinished response. Adding ellipses and stammers everywhere would merely substitute another repeated pattern.

**Use physical beats when they carry a conversational change**

Brandon Sanderson describes establishing concrete context, then using fewer beats as an exchange accelerates. He warns about adding a small action to every line. Kowal sometimes marks intended pauses while drafting and fills in their context later; Dan Wells recommends reading aloud to locate places where delivery needs a beat. [Writing Excuses: Combining Dialogue, Blocking, and Description](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-8-31-combining-dialogue-blocking-and-description/)

For openAuthor, a physical beat should have a reason: controlling access to the tablet, taking time to inspect a result or reacting to a correction. A generic glance, smile or hand movement need not be added to make the page look varied. During quoted-speech-only editing, narration stays locked; variation must come from authorized changes inside speech. A broader draft revision can reconsider existing action placement within its stated scope.

**A bounded experiment**

Freeze one 400–700-word passage containing the recurring pattern and at least one effective ordinary answer. Preserve a separate source inventory of technical facts, knowledge limits, bargain conditions and necessary conversational acts. Record the exact request and change scope before generation.

Compare the current targeted-feedback workflow with the same workflow plus a short preparation instruction: identify the two speakers' immediate purposes, mark the repeated reply pattern and nominate at most three turns for a change in delivery. Each proposed change must explain how it serves the existing purpose and what it retains. Keep the same model, context, call budget and length expectations. Make two fresh attempts per condition, retain every output and include the original passage in the comparison. This is a small development experiment, not a statistical benchmark.

First inspect source fidelity. Then ask blind readers to compare conversational movement, audible rhythm, speaker specificity and enjoyment, allowing a tie or preference for the original. Ask which exact reply changes what the next speaker can do. Read the passage aloud as well. Word-length variation and tactic labels are diagnostics; neither is a quality score. Improvement requires a reader preference without an unacceptable meaning loss, rather than a critic merely approving the candidate.

**Limits**

These are craft tools from particular writers, not universal rules. Rapid symmetrical banter can deliberately express intimacy, rivalry or performance. Direct explanation can be exactly right when one character needs information. Preserving source meaning can also conflict with a request to change the conversational strategy; the author must define which changes are authorized. A preparation map may over-explain motives or force artificial variation, so it should cover only the problematic run and permit keeping a line unchanged. Success on this passage would justify a second test in a different relationship and pressure level, not a claim of generally better dialogue.
